The rk run

The rk run's Q15 results come from epoch 1. They hold inside Q15 fixed point with floor rounding at the run's fixed cycle budget, under the conditions each claim lists. In float64, rk4 is far more accurate than the champion.

Supported: R6, R7, R9, R11, R12, R13, R14. Supported with limits: R1, R2, R3, R4, R5, R8, R10.

R1: The Q15 lead

Supported with limits Headline claim

Claim: Inside Q15 fixed point with floor rounding, at a 65,536-cycle budget, on the analytic m0plus_fast cost model with magnitude weighting, the searched three-stage method 11e898cb has 2.95x lower held-out error than midpoint, the best of eight classical methods.

Limits: This is an epoch-1 result on the cost model that the trace later found had rk4 and rk38 in the wrong order; epoch 2 scores under a corrected model and has not re-measured it. Elites were picked on held-out error among 141,364 archived tableaus, so the four held-out problems act as a selection set and the lead carries winner's-curse bias. The 2.95x is an RMS over four problems: the champion has lower error than midpoint on three of them and higher on pendulum, and much of the gap comes from rc_thermal, where every classical method stalls near a quantization floor. Of six cost-basis and weighting cells, the champion keeps its lead on every leave-one-out subset in two, both analytic: 2.95x under magnitude weighting and 3.36x under reference-norm weighting. On the traced whole-step basis the ratio is 1.01x under magnitude weighting (lowest leave-one-out 0.614x) and 1.84x under reference-norm weighting (lowest 0.604x). Under median-anchor weighting, which weights the four problems equally, it is 1.1x on the analytic basis (lowest 0.814x) and 0.0494x on the traced one; the source reads that last cell as the weighting magnifying pendulum, not as the champion losing by that factor. The lead is a floor-rounding result: under round-to-nearest, at the same budget and cost model and with no extra cycles charged for the rounding, rk38, one of the four classical methods run under both rounding modes, reaches 0.0263 held-out error, below the champion's 0.0286 under floor rounding.

R2: Every discovered cell elite ahead of all eight classical methods

Supported with limits Headline claim

Claim: In all 16 of the 18 occupied archive cells that a discovered method holds, it has lower held-out error than all eight classical methods, whatever their cost; the closest, a four-stage elite at 30 cycles per step, has 0.0836 against midpoint's 0.0844.

Limits: Same conditions and the same selection bias as R1: Q15 with floor rounding, 65,536-cycle budget, analytic cost model, magnitude weighting. Classical methods hold the other 2 cells. Re-ranking all 141,364 archived tableaus on the analytic basis, no classical method ranks above the champion under any of the three weightings, though the champion's own rank moves with the weighting: rank 1 under magnitude weighting, 15 under reference-norm weighting and 9,974 under median-anchor weighting.

R3: Leave-one-out

Supported with limits

Claim: Dropping any one of the four held-out problems leaves the champion ahead, by 1.92x to 3.17x.

Limits: On the published basis only (analytic cost, magnitude weighting). The low end, 1.92x, comes from dropping rc_thermal, the problem where every classical method stalls near a quantization floor. Magnitude weighting lets problems with small errors count for little, so this check cannot show a loss on one of them: the champion has higher error than midpoint on pendulum.

R4: Out-of-sample problems

Supported with limits Headline claim

Claim: On eight out-of-sample problems, the best of the three discovered methods tried had lower Q15 error than the best of the five classical methods run (euler, heun2, midpoint, rk4, rk38) on 4 of 5 non-stiff problems. On the stiff robertson_scaled problem every discovered method overflowed, and so did rk4 and rk38; euler, heun2 and midpoint finished, midpoint with the lowest error.

Limits: Out-of-sample, not blind: no optimizer or model saw these problems and the champion was fixed before they ran, but people chose the problems after the search began. The fixed champion alone had the lower error on 3 of the 5 non-stiff problems.

Evidence

  • rk-work: epochs/1/validation/results.json at verdicts.per_problem; methods; results[]

R5: In float64, rk4 is far more accurate

Supported with limits Headline claim

Claim: In float64, rk4 is far more accurate than the champion: at the step count the 65,536-cycle budget gives each method, the champion's float64 error was 1,992x to 420,975x rk4's on the 7 validation problems where both finish.

Limits: The champion is order 2 and rk4 order 4, and classical order-2 methods trail float64 rk4 by a similar margin in the same runs. Two wider comparisons, run on the seven search and held-out problems rather than the validation set, measure the arithmetic rather than the coefficients: float64 rk4 had lower error than every Q15 run, classical and discovered alike, in 56 of 56 fixed-step cells at identical step counts, and at tolerances of one Q15 least significant bit the most accurate library solver, Radau on each of those problems, was a median 519x more accurate than the most accurate Q15 run. Dormand-Prince (SciPy RK45) was among the library solvers but was never the most accurate one, and it was never run against the champion in the same arithmetic. The search optimized for Q15 floor arithmetic on purpose.

Evidence

  • rk-work: epochs/1/benchmark/results.json at verdicts.fixed_step; verdicts.matched_tolerance; verdicts.per_problem.*.best_library; tolerance_rule

R7: An audit caught the cost model's error

Supported Headline claim

Claim: A host-side audit compiled the step with GCC 13.2.1 and traced it under an emulator. The compiled step agreed with the Python evaluator bit for bit in 144 of 144 cases, 25 of them overflow cases where both stop at the same operation, and it showed that the m0plus_fast cost model the archive was scored under put rk4 and rk38 in the wrong order. The run froze epoch 1 and started epoch 2 under a corrected cost model instead of rescoring the old archive.

Limits: The emulator is instruction-accurate, not cycle-accurate. Cycle counts come from a timing table applied to the executed instructions; nothing was measured on a physical chip.

Evidence

  • rk-work: epochs/1/trace/results.json at verdicts.crosscheck; verdicts.inversions

R9: The premise test came back mixed

Supported

Claim: The project's own premise test, with thresholds committed before the search, came back mixed: rk4 on both cost models and heun2 on the fast one met the proceed criterion, while heun2 on the slow-multiplier model met neither the proceed nor the kill criterion.

Limits: The thresholds were committed to the project's own repository before the test ran, not to an outside registry. A later comparison of four classical methods on the seven search and held-out problems, made after the search began, found rk4 best on 0 of 7 under floor rounding and 1 of 7 under round-to-nearest.

R10: Measured speed and the cost model

Supported with limits

Claim: Measured in Python on the same solver path, the champion's step ran 1.45x faster than rk4's against a model prediction of 1.5x, and analytic cycles tracked measured time with Pearson r = 0.97 over 56 runs.

Limits: Python wall clock, so it speaks to ordering, not to absolute cycle counts on a Cortex-M0+, and only as far as Python mirrors the chip: Python timed rk4 slightly faster than rk38, as the analytic model predicts, while the compiled trace found rk38 cheaper on a Cortex-M0+ (R7).

R12: How the run ran

Supported

Claim: Epoch 1 ran from 2026-08-29 to 2026-09-10: 3,931 search cycles over 13 days of archive and 141,364 scored records, all under the epoch-1 hash apart from 21 written minutes before that pin was set. The 2025 ML project

The project's benchmark ran, and its evaluation harness works for explicit tables, both with limits. Its main conclusions from 2025 did not hold.

Supported: N10, N11. Supported with limits: N1, N9. Not supported: N2, N3, N4, N7, N8. Artifact: N5, N6.

N1: The 2025 benchmark ran

Supported with limits Headline claim

Claim: Across 12 methods and 10,000 generated ODEs (8,113 scorable), Dormand-Prince had the lowest mean error (0.0791) and RK4 the next lowest (0.14).

Limits: The error is the mean of each ODE's maximum error, which a few outlier ODEs dominate. The trial entries were copies of RK4 and Dormand-Prince or random tables, the Gauss-Legendre entries ran through a stepper that ignores the implicit part of a table (N5), and the reference solutions came from SciPy RK45, an adaptive Dormand-Prince solver, so the Dormand-Prince entry is measured against a reference built on its own tableau. This ranks two textbook methods against copies of themselves, random tables and truncated methods.

Related claims: N5

Evidence

  • Novel-Numerical-Integration-Methods: results/real_trial_results.csv at Max_Error

N2: The rediscovery claim (not supported)

Not supported Headline claim

Claim: Evolutionary search independently rediscovered RK4 and Dormand-Prince.

Original wording:

Evolutionary search, by contrast, independently rediscovered RK4 and Dormand-Prince with exact performance match (README).

Correction: RK4 was placed in the starting population of the 4-stage runs and Dormand-Prince in that of the 7-stage run. The genetic operators did run and the population moved off the seed, but the composite score is clipped at 1: every one of the 10,000 rows of the one training log scores 1, and the checkpoints of trials 12 to 15 record a best and a mean score of 1 in every one of their 100 epochs. The best-table tracker replaces the saved table only on a strictly higher score, so the seed stayed. Trials 12, 14 and 15 saved RK4 exactly and trial 13 saved Dormand-Prince exactly.

N4: The local-minima claim (not supported)

Not supported Headline claim

Claim: Gradient descent collapsed into local minima.

Original wording:

Empirical benchmarks show gradient descent consistently collapsed into local minima, producing near-identical low-accuracy schemes (README).

Correction: The generator that proposes tables was never trained: its optimizer is created and never stepped, its outputs never pass validation, and every empty slot is filled by a random generator that resets the seed to 42. The only gradient descent in the project trained a surrogate model, and nothing in the training loop reads its predictions. Trials 1-4, 6, 8 and 9 saved the same A matrix and weights b (trials 8 and 9 carry a different node vector c, which none of the 6 test families depends on). Trials 8 and 9 score the same because they are the same table.

N5: Gauss-Legendre 3's error (an artifact)

Artifact

Claim: Gauss-Legendre 3 is numerically unstable, with a mean error of 8.31e+211.

Original wording:

Gauss-Legendre 3: Numerical instability (8.31e+211 max error) (results/analysis_report.md).

Correction: The stepper reads the diagonal only while that stage is still zero and never reads the upper triangle, so an implicit table runs as its strictly lower part. Both Gauss-Legendre entries ran as truncated explicit methods of order one; the number measures that bug, not the method.

Evidence

  • Novel-Numerical-Integration-Methods: src/core/integrator_runner.py at 201-214

N9: The 2025 harness

Supported with limits

Claim: The project built a working evaluation harness for explicit tables: a generic Butcher-table stepper, a generator covering 11 ODE families including stiff ones, a SciPy reference solver, parallel scoring and 16 trial configurations, and it ran a 12-method benchmark over 10,000 ODEs. Its source tree holds 10,279 lines of Python across 47 files.

Limits: The harness runs end to end for explicit tables. Two of its parts produced the artifacts in N5 and N6: the stepper runs only the explicit part of a table, and the reference solver covers 5 of the 6 test families and 5 of the 11 training families. An SQLite results module is in the tree, but nothing in the pipeline calls it. Its main conclusions from 2025 are what did not hold.

Related claims: N5, N6

Across both projects

Each of these draws on both projects and names the claims above that it rests on.

Supported with limits: U1. Not supported: U2, U3.

U1: Which methods lead depends on the arithmetic and the cost model

Supported with limits Headline claim

Claim: In float64 the established methods were more accurate in both projects: in the rk run float64 rk4 was 1,992x to 420,975x more accurate than the champion at the same cycle budget, and in the 2025 benchmark Dormand-Prince and RK4 had the lowest mean error.

Limits: The 2025 benchmark is weak evidence: its other entries were copies of RK4 and Dormand-Prince, random tables and truncated methods, and its reference solutions came from SciPy RK45, itself a Dormand-Prince solver (N1). In the rk run Dormand-Prince was never run against the champion in the same arithmetic (R5). Inside Q15 with floor rounding at a 65,536-cycle budget, on the analytic cost model, the searched method leads the classical fixed-step methods (R1); on the traced whole-step basis that lead falls to 1.01x, which dropping one held-out problem overturns.

Related claims: N1, R5, R1

Evidence
